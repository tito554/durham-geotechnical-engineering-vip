Electrical Resistivity Testing & VES Surveys in Durham, NC

Together, we solve the challenges of tomorrow.

Durham's transformation from a tobacco manufacturing hub into a center for research and medicine has driven rapid redevelopment across varied Triassic Basin terrain. The underlying geology shifts quickly: deep weathered saprolite over diabase sills, interspersed with Triassic sedimentary rocks of the Durham sub-basin. When a new life sciences building rises near the American Tobacco District or a parking structure expands on Ninth Street, the first question our lab team asks is where the competent bedrock really sits. Electrical resistivity surveys answer that without driving a single borehole. By injecting a measured current through the ground and reading the potential difference, we construct a vertical profile of subsurface resistivity that reveals lithologic boundaries, water saturation zones, and potential karst features hidden beneath the Piedmont clay. Before committing to expensive SPT drilling, a VES line gives us the stratigraphic picture we need to plan the investigation efficiently.

A resistivity jump from 30 to 400 Ohm-m across a single AB/2 spacing often marks the exact transition from weathered saprolite to competent diabase in the Durham sub-basin.

Process overview

Durham sits at roughly 400 feet elevation on the Piedmont Plateau, but the water table here can fluctuate dramatically between the shallow weathered zone and deeper fractured bedrock. We run vertical electrical sounding using a Schlumberger array with expanding electrode spacing, typically from AB/2=1.5 m out to 150 m or more, depending on target depth. The key output is a resistivity-versus-depth curve that we invert with software matching the ASTM D6431 workflow. What we see most often in Durham: a low-resistivity clay-rich saprolite layer (15-50 Ohm-m) overlying a transitional weathered rock zone (50-200 Ohm-m), then a sharp jump to competent bedrock above 300 Ohm-m. That jump is what structural engineers need. For karst-prone areas near the Eno River, low-resistivity anomalies can indicate clay-filled cavities, while air-filled voids spike the apparent resistivity. We also run parallel 2D profiles when lateral variability matters, and the resistivity data feeds directly into seismic refraction interpretations where we need both electrical and mechanical stratigraphy tied to the same site grid.

Local context

Much of central Durham sits atop the Durham sub-basin, where Triassic-age sandstones and mudstones alternate with diabase intrusions. The contact between these units is rarely planar: weathering can penetrate 30 to 80 feet deep along fracture zones, creating erratic bedrock profiles that mislead single-point investigations. The biggest risk we see is misinterpreting a large diabase boulder within saprolite as the top of competent rock. Resistivity data catches this because the boulder shows as a localized high-resistivity anomaly rather than a sustained shift in the curve. Karst is less common here than in the Shenandoah Valley, but the carbonate rocks of the nearby Efland Formation extend into western Durham County, and solution cavities do appear. A missed void under a column footing means differential settlement that shows up within the first year of service. Our VES protocol includes multiple sounding points per site, calibrated against at least one borehole or test pit for lithologic ground truth, so the resistivity model reflects actual subsurface conditions.

Quick answers

How deep can a resistivity survey see in Durham's Piedmont soils?

Depth of investigation depends on the maximum current electrode spacing. With AB/2 reaching 150 to 200 meters, we typically resolve features down to 50-65 meters in the weathered Piedmont profile. The limiting factor is usually site geometry, not the equipment, since the array needs open ground to extend the electrodes. For deeper targets we shift to time-domain IP or controlled-source audio-magnetotellurics.

Can resistivity tell the difference between diabase and Triassic sandstone?

Yes, and this is one of the most useful applications in Durham. Fresh diabase typically measures above 300 Ohm-m and often exceeds 500 Ohm-m. Triassic sandstones and mudstones of the Durham sub-basin sit in the 80 to 200 Ohm-m range when fresh, dropping lower when clay-rich. The contrast shows clearly on the inverted resistivity section, which helps us map the intrusive contacts that control groundwater flow and foundation bearing conditions.

What does a VES survey cost for a typical residential lot in Durham?

For a standard single-family lot with two to three sounding points and a written report including interpreted bedrock profiles, the cost ranges from US$640 to US$1.160 depending on access conditions and the maximum depth required. Deeper investigations or sites with heavy vegetation that slows field setup fall toward the upper end of that range.

Do we still need boreholes if we run a resistivity survey?

Almost always, but resistivity reduces the number of boreholes and makes them smarter. The geophysical data tells us where to drill to hit the contacts we need to verify, rather than guessing on a grid. Typically one borehole per resistivity line provides the lithologic calibration to convert resistivity values into actual rock types, and that combination satisfies both IBC geotechnical investigation requirements and the curiosity of a good engineering geologist.
